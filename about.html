The idea

About

Short first-person accounts of service, duty and comradeship — not a parade, and not a politics lesson.

Life is hard. It is meant to be so. The difficulty is not an aberration but the very condition that confers worth upon achievement. Success lightly won is success lightly valued; that which is wrested from adversity carries a deeper savour. Failure, too, will come — sometimes repeatedly. This is neither tragedy nor indictment.

The only true failure is the abandonment of the struggle itself. To persist, to rise again, to refuse the counsel of despair: these are the lessons that endure, and they are best learned not from theory but from those who have practised them under fire or in the long, unglamorous grind of duty.

In a country growing ever more multiracial, the shared language of belonging becomes more, not less, essential. Without it, the mosaic risks becoming a mere heap of fragments. Civic pride and patriotism are not relics of a vanished age; they are the mortar that still binds.

How a session works

Veterans of the British Armed Forces give short first-person accounts — typically ten or fifteen minutes each. The tone is unpretentious: tea and a bacon roll in ordinary surroundings when we meet at a pub; a classroom or library when hosts invite us in.

We start in Oxfordshire. The aim is national. The work is supported by a Civic Future Action Grant.

Yours sincerely,
Rory Hanrahan
